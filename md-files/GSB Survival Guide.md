Welcome. You are holding (or scrolling) the accumulated, argued-over, road-tested advice of a class of Stanford GSB students — pulled together from a year of WhatsApp threads, Slack posts, OneNote pages, handouts, and hallway conversations, then cleaned up and organized so you don't have to learn everything the hard way.
The guide follows the arc of your time here, in three parts:
Part I — Before You Arrive. Packing, getting here, housing, insurance, money, and phones. Read this the summer before you land.
Part II — Life at the GSB. The big middle: your room, food, tech, communication channels, academics, careers, social life, trips, health, getting around, local services, significant others, international students, and looking after yourself. Skim it early, then come back to chapters as they become relevant.
Part III — Exiting the GSB. The summer between years, second year, and graduation — the things everyone wishes they had known three months earlier.
A companion Resources folder holds the most useful original attach-ments (a health-insurance comparison spreadsheet, a packing list, the Blast FAQ, and several official PDFs). It lives in a public Google Drive folder — open to anyone with the link, no Stanford login needed — and every reference to it in this guide is a direct link to the file itself. Appendix A lists everything in it.
Short on time? Each part now opens with an Essentials page — the handful of things to read if you read nothing else. And the fastest route of all: feed this guide into an AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini — the PDF works well) and simply ask it questions. It will answer with the guide’s specifics and point you to the right chapter.
(And if you want it on paper: any print shop will spiral-bind the digestsized PDF for about fifteen dollars. It makes a decent gift for an admit.)

Conventions and caveats
Attribution. Advice drawn from named, passed-down guides — an author's reflection, a resource list with its maker's name on it — is credited inline. Advice from the class's group chats appears uncited by design; a collective thank-you lives in the Acknow-ledgments — with apologies to anyone missed.
Prices and dates. Anything with a dollar sign was true in the 2025–26 academic year (or is marked with its year) and will drift. One-off events and dated deadlines have been cut; recurring patterns (“registration opens in late February”) are kept as patterns, not promises.
Links. Hyperlinks are preserved throughout — including long Amazon product links and passed-down Google Docs, on the theory that a working link beats a pretty one. Many require a SUNet login; a few will inevitably rot — search MyGSB for the current page when one does. Class-specific links (forms, spreadsheets, chat invites) are kept as models of what each class rebuilds every year.
A word on accuracy. This guide was sourced from a great deal of information and guidance, compiled at a single point in time. The vast majority should be accurate — but if anything seems confusing or off, verify it against the GSB Student Support office, your peer mentors, and current MBA2s, who hold the most up-to-date knowledge. Many linked pages get updated (and some don't) — check the date on anything official before relying on it. External references are used throughout as sources, but it's never a perfect science.
On completeness. These tips were deliberately compiled to in-clude more ideas rather than fewer, so you get many students’ takes side by side. Not every tip has been personally tested by the author, and plenty are individual opinions — take each for what it is.

← S →A Brief History of the Farm (and the GSB)
Start with the university. In 1885, Leland and Jane Stanford founded a university on their Palo Alto stock farm in memory of their only son, Leland Jr., who had died of typhoid at fifteen — declaring that “the children of California shall be our children.” The horse farm gave the campus its permanent nickname: the Farm. Stanford opened on Octo-ber 1, 1891 — coeducational and non-denominational from the first day, both radical choices for the era — nearly went under after Leland Sr.'s death (Jane Stanford famously kept it alive through the lean years, reportedly ready to sell her jewels to pay the faculty), was shaken hard by the 1906 earthquake, and then spent the twentieth century becoming the intellectual engine of the region. When Frederick Terman began encouraging his engineering students to start companies nearby rather than go East, the orchards around the Farm started turning into what the world now calls Silicon Valley.
So much for the university. Now to the grove you'll live in.
It helps to know what you're joining. In 1925, trustee Herbert Hoover — two years before the presidency — assembled a committee of Califor-nia businessmen (Wallace Alexander, George Rolph, Paul Shoup, Thomas Gregory, and Milton Esberg) to fund a business school on the Farm, largely so the West's ambitious wouldn't keep decamping East for their training. Willard Hotchkiss became the first dean, and on Octo-ber 1, 1925, the Stanford Graduate School of Business opened with sixteen students in borrowed classrooms next to the biology department — only the second school in America to offer the MBA. (The GSB's own history; the centennial timeline)
The century between then and you runs through the case method's arrival, the post-war quantitative turn, and the school's slow inversion of the East Coast hierarchy it was built to escape. How Stanford built Silicon Valley
Know the ground you're standing on, because Stanford didn't just end up near Silicon Valley — it more or less created it. The prime mover was Frederick Terman, the engineering dean and later provost often called the father of Silicon Valley, whose radical idea in the 1930s and '40s was to push his students to start companies near Stanford rather than decamp to the East Coast. His most famous protégés, Bill Hewlett and Dave Packard, did exactly that: Hewlett-Packard was formalized on January 1, 1939, out of a tiny Palo Alto garage now plaqued as the “Birthplace of Silicon Valley.” In 1951 Terman went further and created the Stanford Industrial Park (today the Stanford Research Park) — the world's first university research park — leasing Stanford land to firms like Varian and HP and pulling the whole industry to the university's doorstep. The other genetic strand arrived in 1956, when transistor coinventor William Shockley set up shop in Mountain View; a year later eight of his best people (the “traitorous eight,” among them Gordon Moore and Robert Noyce) walked out to found Fairchild Semiconductor, and Moore and Noyce later left Fairchild to start Intel. The silicon those companies etched is why the journalist Don Hoefler popularized the name “Silicon Valley” in print in 1971. (Computer History Museum; Stanford Engineering)

What to actually bring
Pack what is expensive, hard to replace, or needed the day you arrive — plus the personal stuff (photos, small decorations) that makes a dorm room feel like yours. Nearly everything else can be bought within a 15-minute drive or delivered in two days.
Clothes. The GSB dress code is very relaxed — t-shirts and ath-leisure are the norm. Bring formal outfits for interviews and formal GSB events. The unofficial wardrobe calendar: Halloween costumes in fall; something sparkly for Vegas FOAM in winter; formalwear (long gowns, suits) for the Winter Formal in winter and GSB Prom in spring; and national dress or costumes for the many themed and costume parties in between.
A warm layer. Despite the sunny brochures, Palo Alto gets cold from November to February, especially at night. Pack sweaters and a real coat.
Medication and prescriptions. Bring a refill of any prescrip-tions so there's no pharmacy scramble on arrival, and a paper copy of your eyeglass prescription — eye exams in the US are expensive.
Sports gear. Tahoe is three hours away, Santa Cruz has surf, and the redwoods have world-class trails. If you own gear you love, bring it — campus sports infrastructure is extensive.
Electronics (international students): bring at least one US-mains adapter for day one; consider replacing cheap electronics in the US rather than hauling a bag of adapters.

GSB Residences rooms come with more than you might expect. ← S →2. Getting to Campus
Most people fly into SFO (closer, more international routes) or SJC (of-ten calmer and closer to the South Bay). Your options from the airport, in rough order of popularity:

Pre-MBA trips (for the admitted-student reader)
The big three are Yacht Week, Colombia, and pre-MBA Tahoe. One classmate enjoyed doing all three: it's pretty special to travel with a hundred-plus of your future classmates, and you're simply never going to get that in the real world. If your summer allows the time, enjoy it before school starts.

Cardinal Care — the default
All students are automatically enrolled in Cardinal Care, Stanford's student health plan (administered through Aetna networks). It is comprehensive and expensive, and enrollment is a full-year commitment that runs through the end of August — there are no early-cancellation refunds, even if your summer employer covers you. If you want out, you must apply for a waiver by the posted deadline (mid-September for autumn entrants); the health office does not grant exceptions to the deadline. Start at the Vaden insurance overview.
One rule that bites people: Cardinal Care (underwritten by Well-fleet) now strictly enforces a referral requirement — as of 2026, nonemergency specialty and off-campus care generally must be referred through Vaden first, or the claim can be denied. So unless it's an emergency, start at Vaden or its advice line and get the referral before you book an outside specialist. When something's unclear, open a support request through Stanford's help system to ask or appeal.

Get a US number working before Week Zero — you will meet hundreds of people, and every list, group chat, delivery, and twofactor prompt needs a US number. Carrier stores cluster on Uni-versity Avenue and El Camino Real near California Avenue (bring your passport if you're international).

The 10-minute US phone-plan primer
Three national networks — Verizon (the traditional “premi-um” network), AT&T (vies for the top slot; historically the internationals' favorite because it didn't demand SSNs), and T-Mobile (cheapest unlimited plans, weakest in fringe spots). Every plan is voice + text + data; nearly all have unlimited voice/text and compete on data, price, and perks (Wi-Fi calling, international roaming).
MVNOs (Mint Mobile, Google Fi, Cricket, and dozens more) re-sell the big networks' towers under cheaper brands. Mint Mo-bile supports Wi-Fi calling and text — useful given campus dead zones — and MVNO SIMs can be ordered online and even shipped to a friend before you arrive.

The Blast Tax
One piece of Blast lore you'll meet fast. In 2008 a second-year named Amal Dorai, drowning in some fifty blasts a day, sent a half-joking email calculating that each all-school blast cost the community about $54 of “expected utility” — and proposed that anyone sending one should “pay it back” by adding something genuinely funny or interesting at the end. The quarter system, in brief
If you're coming from a semester school, recalibrate: Stanford runs on quarters. There are three ten-week terms — Autumn (late September to mid-December), Winter (January to mid-March), and Spring (late March to early June) — each capped by what Stanford primly calls the “End-Quarter Period” (finals; everywhere else it's dead week). Ten weeks is fast: midterms land around week five and a class is over almost before it began, which is part of why the place feels intense and why you can sample so much. The MBA runs September to June, twice.

Choosing classes: one classmate's philosophy
Sit near the front — better interaction with professors, guest speakers, and students, and early seat-assignment claims.
Prefer the 30–40-person seminar over the 80-person mega-class when you can get it: the back-and-forth with practi-tioners is where the value hides, and taking a professor's class is the single best way to build a real relationship with them.
Take classes OUTSIDE your comfort sector. If you're a healthcare person, the intro healthcare classes teach you little and you already have the contacts; take real estate or climate instead — low-baseline knowledge compounds fastest, and those classmates may be the only contacts you ever get in that industry.
Mind the group-work load. Classes built on mandatory group projects lock you into the group’s pace — fine when that’s the point, but they take away your ability to dial a course up or down to fit the rest of your quarter.
And the taxonomy of why anyone takes any class, one MBA's taxonomy: GPA-maxer, coaster, learner, prestige-maxxer, professor-hunter. Know which one you're being on each pick — any is fine, in moderation.

Grades, pass/fail, and staying in good standing
Grades appear in Axess; your unofficial transcript is available there anytime, automatically.
The folklore, recorded so you can verify it: of the 90 units to graduate, up to 10 can be pass/fail (GSB or ATS), and up to 10 can be ATS units (P/F or letter). ATS letter grades don't flow into your GSB GPA. The strategic corollary the class converged on: save your P/F budget for GSB classes and take ATS classes for letter grades. Accidentally P/F-ing an ATS class can burn both budgets at once. Confirm all of it against the registrar before betting your graduation math on it.
The Good Academic Standing requirements are published per class year. If you're worried about a grade, the Student Sup-port office (gsb_mbastudentsupport@stanford.edu) wants to hear from you early.
The U-grade rules, per an official Student Support reply: one U in an elective doesn't cost you good standing but earns no units; a single U in a core/Management Foundations course does cost good standing, and (after successfully petitioning back in) the course must be retaken; more than one U anywhere, or a GSB GPA below 2.65, also ends good standing.
Attendance: policies live in each syllabus and professors mean them to varying degrees. The formal absence report tool exists; in practice most people simply email the professor and TA per the syllabus policy. Watch for hard lines — at least one popular elective's syllabus makes missing the last session grounds for a U. Plan end-of-quarter travel around final sessions and presentations (some in-person finals run four hours).

The grade scale and the Arjay Miller Scholars
For the record, the GSB grade scale has four tiers — Honors, High Pass, Pass, and Low Pass (H / HP / P / LP) — with a recommended forced curve that pushes most people to Pass (roughly 10% Honors, 30% High Pass, 50% Pass, 10% Low Pass). Pair that with the class's strong grade non-disclosure norm — students don't share grades and recruiters famously don't ask — and you get the local culture where grades recede. The one place they resurface: the top ten percent of each graduating class are named Arjay Miller Scholars, the school's oldest academic honor. Because of the forced curve and the non-disclosure norm, it's quietly one of the hardest distinctions to earn here. Worth knowing the term; not worth reorganizing your two years around.

The search fund: Stanford's signature entrepreneurial path
If there is one kind of entrepreneurship the GSB is the global home of, it's the search fund — “entrepreneurship through acquisition.” The idea was conceived here in 1984: rather than found a company from scratch, you raise a small fund from a dozen-odd investors to spend up to two years searching for one good small business to buy, then raise the acquisition capital, buy it, and run it as CEO. It's a remarkably fast route to actually operating a company with real ownership, and it's a serious, well-trodden path at the GSB — with its own club, course, and investor network. The school's Search Fund Primer (from the Grousbeck-Hollo-way Center for Entrepreneurial Studies) is the movement's founding text, and a 2024 GSB study of 681 North American search funds found an aggregate pre-tax return of about 35% a year. Even if you never run one, understanding the model explains a whole slice of your classmates. (the GSB's search-fund page)

FOAM membership: buy or not?
The honest math, from a classmate who ran the numbers: (1) without a membership you can't attend several of the big FOAM events — they have actual security, and not just Vegas and LA but Band and other capacity-limited ones too; (2) the price is the same whenever you buy, so waiting just raises your per-event cost; (3) even if you don't drink, most people find it worth it if you'll attend ~2 major FOAMs a year, given venue and food costs — an easier case still if you use the open bars. TL;DR: unless you're impervious to FOMO, do it.
The story behind the name. FOAM stands for “Friends of Arjay Miller” — the beloved dean who ran the GSB from 1969 to 1979. Miller was a former president of Ford Motor Company who, as dean, used to turn up at student spaghetti dinners; admiring students started a drinking club in his honor, and the name stuck. Confusingly, “Arjay Miller” also names the school's top academic honor: the top ten percent of each graduating class are named Arjay Miller Scholars. Same man, two very different distinctions. A word on money, because it matters. The headline experiences here — Vegas FOAM, the Coachella pilgrimage, the far-flung treks — are wonderful and expensive, and it's easy to feel you have to do all of them. You don't. Some of the very best of this place costs little or nothing: the talks, the dinners, TALK, office hours, a sunrise walk up the Dish, a potluck, a night in someone's backyard. Plenty of people have a rich, full two years on a fraction of the big-ticket spend, and the community is better when no one feels priced out — spend where it's worth it to you, skip the rest without apology, and know the financial-aid office and the relief valves in Chapter 5 are there for exactly this.
Vegas FOAM, tactically
Stay at the host hotel — or one nearby. Booking sites' cheap-er options carry hidden resort fees that erase the difference, and logistics get much easier when the party is an elevator ride away.

The GSB club directory: a snapshot
The GSB runs on student clubs — roughly seventy-five of them, and the roster shifts a little each year. This is a snapshot of the school's official directory; join generously in the fall, since most cost little and ask nothing but your email. Speakers: who walks through the door
One of the quiet luxuries of this place is who turns up on an ordinary Monday. The Dean's flagship series, View From The Top (VFTT), has run since 1978 and brings a CEO or world figure to campus for a lunchtime conversation — in recent years interviewed by one of your own classmates. Tickets are free but capacity-limited and go by lottery; there is often a separate lottery for a smaller sit-down lunch. Beyond VFTT, the clubs run a near-constant stream of fireside chats and class visitors, and some electives are built around guest operators who show up in the room. Show up even when you're busy — the density is the whole point.

Global Study Trips (GST): how the lottery actually works
First, what they are: GSTs are faculty-led, week-long deep-dive industry visits to another country — not tourism, but privileged ac-cess to the business infrastructure underneath a place: companies, ministries, founders, and operators you could never cold-email your way into. They are routinely the highlight of people's entire GSB experience. Highly recommended — and rank every country that interests you.
Confirmed with the GST office, because misinformation abounds: every student gets a random lottery number (say, 1 to 450). The algorithm processes students lowest number first; for each student it checks their choice 1, then 2, then 3, assigning the first trip with space. It does not matter whether a trip is your first or fourth choice — if your turn comes and it has spots, you get it. Therefore:
There is no advantage to ranking fewer trips. If your only two ranked trips fill before your number comes up, you simply get nothing; waitpools open afterwards but spots are rare — don't depend on them.
Rank every trip you'd actually take, weighing topic, location, and dates — this lottery is the only moment all trips are open.
Once allocated you're committed and enrolled: a $350 nonrefundable deposit immediately, roughly $3,000 all-in (2025–26 figures), plus prep and participation time. There's a ~2-day drop window after allocation emails, and you must state the circumstance and how you'll otherwise meet the Global Ex-perience Requirement (GER). Cancelling later means owing the full cost unless there's a major exception (e.g., medical) — not all GSTs sell out, so there may be no waitlist taker for your spot. The office works with genuine health cases.

Worth doing even if you rarely drive: it's the primary US ID (no more passport at bars), it lowers car insurance, and out-of-state licenses eventually annoy you. A classmate's study notes cover exactly what's tested; the free DMV handbook does the rest. Passing gets you a supervised-driving permit and the right to book the road test.

What I'd do differently — an MBA2's list
Celeste Bean wrote and shared a reflection at the end of her first year that was very well received in the class. Her eleven points, condensed here (the full document is worth your fifteen minutes):
Think critically about AI in your education. You learn nothing tossing every assignment into a chatbot; the point of assignments is the uncomfortable experience of not understanding. Use AI to use your own brain better — clarifying questions you'd be embarrassed to ask a person, organizing ideas into frameworks, finding gaps by explaining concepts back to it.

Uninstall Instagram sooner (or, respectfully, mute your classmates).

General lessons (Nikhil Jain's personal notes and opinions)
A caveat before these: this is simply how I, personally, came to approach Stanford. Everyone has their own journey, preferences, and circumstances, and yours will rightly differ — I share them only in case they're useful.
Think from abundance. Most problems here are problems of abundance — too many good things pulling at once. That's not a problem; that's the point. There will always be another party, another dinner, another BBL.
Maybe 10% of what's good at Stanford is at the GSB. Clubs, classes, and events across medicine, engineering, design, and law are what your tuition actually buys. Check in on your international classmates, especially early — the transition is heavier than it looks from outside, and a check-in costs nothing.

The dips, on schedule
One more layer on this calendar, offered as scheduled honesty: the morale dips here are so predictable you can nearly circle them in advance — and every class discovers them one person at a time, each privately convinced it’s just them. It isn’t. Around week five of autumn, the arrival adrenaline wears off mid-midterms and mid-recruiting, and the first real wobble hits: the “everyone here is smarter and betterconnected than me” week. It passes. February brings the long-middle slump — gray weather by California standards, the winter formals behind you, internships unsettled, autumn’s novelty gone. It passes too. And in spring of MBA2 year, the pre-goodbye melancholy arrives right on time.
None of this is a personal failing; it’s the syllabus. So plan for the dips the way you’d plan for finals: something on the February calendar worth looking forward to, standing time with people who knew you before Stanford, and Chapter 17’s support map within reach. The truth about internships
There is no school requirement to do an internship — none. People build startups, travel, take real sabbaticals, and do GMIX projects. One caveat with teeth: if you want GSB summer funding (SMIF, ESIP, EcoSIP), the supported engagement must run at least eight weeks — and stacking a GMIX or Seed project on top of eight weeks is about the limit of sanity.
Know the funding programs early — many people regret not doing so. The GSB runs five or six programs that fund low-paid intern-ships or your own entrepreneurial project (you can use one CES/CSI-funded program once in your degree). SMIF in particular subsidizes in-ternships at organizations that couldn't otherwise pay you — which opens a whole tier of startup, nonprofit, and fund roles. “I only found out about this around March... if I had known earlier, it might have changed how I approached internship searching. It really does open up a whole set of more informal opportunities.” The official program handout (2025 edition) is in the Resources folder; ask the CES/CSI teams for the current one, and for ESIP specifically, the program staff give excellent 1:1s.

Second year runs on a different scarcity. As an MBA1 the constraint was information — which classes, which trips, which people. As an MBA2 it’s intention: you know how the machine works now, and the only question is what you do with your last pass through it.
Setting up the year
Housing renewal mostly works. Students who held their lease through the summer generally renewed the same unit; nobody who wanted to stay in the midrises was known to be forced out. Group applications: everyone applies, one leader forms the group, the group rides the leader's priority. Selection runs late April/early May.

← S →A Closing Note: On Contribution
I made this guide for a simple reason: I love this school, and I love what it looks like when people give back to it. So let the last words here be about contribution — because the best thing about the GSB is not what it can do for you. Ask instead what you can do for the GSB: for your community, your peers, the people around you.
Culture doesn't appear on its own. Somebody organizes the thing nobody else wants to organize, starts the group chat, sets the theme, writes the guide, welcomes the internationals, checks on the classmate having a hard week. It's real work and often thankless — and the thanklessness is the point. Doing it anyway, for the collective, is the service. As former Student Association president Yusef Houamed put it: caring is cool.
So here's what I'd ask of you: choose generosity over selfishness. Kindness over calculation. Community over individualism. Open exchange over closed silos. Contribution over extraction. Make the people around you friends, not contacts. Thank people loudly and often — genuine thanks happen far less than expected and land far harder. Assume good intent, and be visible proof that genuine service exists. Be a school where anyone can sit with anyone at Arbuckle.
And look out for the class below you — it's contribution in its purest form here, because they haven't had their shot yet and there's nothing competitive about it. Everything in this book was handed to you by people you'll mostly never meet. Make the next class feel so welcomed that they want to do even better for the one after that.
Welcome to the Stanford Graduate School of Business. I hope you make the most of these wonderful two years.
— Nikhil Jain

Section full but another isn't? Same-class section switches go through the regular switching system; if seats are open, just drop/re-add on Axess.
Need proof of enrollment fast? The unofficial transcript on Axess generates instantly.
New course, no reviews anywhere? Professors often post an intro video on the registration site — check before passing.
One underrated GSB elective the chats kept naming? Fin-ancial Restructuring.
