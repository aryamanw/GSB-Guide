## How to Use This Guide

Welcome. You are holding (or scrolling) the accumulated, argued-over, road-tested advice of a class of Stanford GSB students — pulled together from a year of WhatsApp threads, Slack posts, OneNote pages, handouts, and hallway conversations, then cleaned up and organized so you don't have to learn everything the hard way[cite: 1].

The guide follows the arc of your time here, in three parts[cite: 1]:
- **Part I — Before You Arrive:** Packing, getting here, housing, insurance, money, and phones. Read this the summer before you land[cite: 1].
- **Part II — Life at the GSB:** The big middle: your room, food, tech, communication channels, academics, careers, social life, trips, health, getting around, local services, significant others, international students, and looking after yourself. Skim it early, then come back to chapters as they become relevant[cite: 1].
- **Part III — Exiting the GSB:** The summer between years, second year, and graduation — the things everyone wishes they had known three months earlier[cite: 1].

A companion **Resources folder** holds the most useful original attachments (a health-insurance comparison spreadsheet, a packing list, the Blast FAQ, and several official PDFs)[cite: 1]. It lives in a public Google Drive folder — open to anyone with the link, no Stanford login needed — and every reference to it in this guide is a direct link to the file itself[cite: 1]. Appendix A lists everything in it[cite: 1].

Short on time? Each part opens with an **Essentials** page — the handful of things to read if you read nothing else[cite: 1]. And the fastest route of all: feed this guide into an AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini — the PDF works well) and simply ask it questions[cite: 1]. It will answer with the guide’s specifics and point you to the right chapter[cite: 1].

*(And if you want it on paper: any print shop will spiral-bind the digest-sized PDF for about fifteen dollars. It makes a decent gift for an admit.)*[cite: 1]

### Conventions and Caveats
- **Attribution:** Advice drawn from named, passed-down guides — an author's reflection, a resource list with its maker's name on it — is credited inline[cite: 1]. Advice from the class's group chats appears uncited by design; a collective thank-you lives in the Acknowledgments — with apologies to anyone missed[cite: 1].
- **Prices and Dates:** Anything with a dollar sign was true in the 2025–26 academic year (or is marked with its year) and will drift[cite: 1]. One-off events and dated deadlines have been cut; recurring patterns (“registration opens in late February”) are kept as patterns, not promises[cite: 1].
- **Links:** Hyperlinks are preserved throughout — including long Amazon product links and passed-down Google Docs, on the theory that a working link beats a pretty one[cite: 1]. Many require a SUNet login; a few will inevitably rot — search MyGSB for the current page when one does[cite: 1]. Class-specific links (forms, spreadsheets, chat invites) are kept as models of what each class rebuilds every year[cite: 1].
- **A Word on Accuracy:** This guide was sourced from a great deal of information and guidance, compiled at a single point in time[cite: 1]. The vast majority should be accurate — but if anything seems confusing or off, verify it against the GSB Student Support office, your peer mentors, and current MBA2s, who hold the most up-to-date knowledge[cite: 1]. Many linked pages get updated (and some don't) — check the date on anything official before relying on it[cite: 1]. External references are used throughout as sources, but it's never a perfect science[cite: 1].
- **On Completeness:** These tips were deliberately compiled to include more ideas rather than fewer, so you get many students’ takes side by side[cite: 1]. Not every tip has been personally tested by the author, and plenty are individual opinions — take each for what it is[cite: 1].

## A Brief History of the Farm (and the GSB)

Start with the university[cite: 1]. In 1885, Leland and Jane Stanford founded a university on their Palo Alto stock farm in memory of their only son, Leland Jr., who had died of typhoid at fifteen — declaring that “the children of California shall be our children.”[cite: 1] The horse farm gave the campus its permanent nickname: **the Farm**[cite: 1]. Stanford opened on October 1, 1891 — coeducational and non-denominational from the first day, both radical choices for the era — nearly went under after Leland Sr.'s death (Jane Stanford famously kept it alive through the lean years, reportedly ready to sell her jewels to pay the faculty), was shaken hard by the 1906 earthquake, and then spent the twentieth century becoming the intellectual engine of the region[cite: 1]. When Frederick Terman began encouraging his engineering students to start companies nearby rather than go East, the orchards around the Farm started turning into what the world now calls Silicon Valley[cite: 1].

So much for the university[cite: 1]. Now to the grove you'll live in[cite: 1].

It helps to know what you're joining[cite: 1]. In 1925, trustee Herbert Hoover — two years before the presidency — assembled a committee of California businessmen (Wallace Alexander, George Rolph, Paul Shoup, Thomas Gregory, and Milton Esberg) to fund a business school on the Farm, largely so the West's ambitious wouldn't keep decamping East for their training[cite: 1]. Willard Hotchkiss became the first dean, and on October 1, 1925, the Stanford Graduate School of Business opened with sixteen students in borrowed classrooms next to the biology department — only the second school in America to offer the MBA[cite: 1].

The century between then and you runs through the case method's arrival, the post-war quantitative turn, and the school's slow inversion of the East Coast hierarchy it was built to escape[cite: 1]. The campus you'll live on is new by comparison: in 2006, Phil Knight, MBA '62, gave $105 million — then the largest gift ever made to a business school — toward the $375 million, 12.5-acre Knight Management Center, opened in 2011[cite: 1]. The school marked its centennial on October 10, 2025 — reunions and panels in the same Town Square you'll cross daily[cite: 1].

Why it matters practically: the GSB's defining choice is smallness — a class of ~430 in a university of 17,000, on a campus built for conversation rather than lecture[cite: 1]. Most of this guide's advice is downstream of that one design decision, made a hundred years ago and never reversed[cite: 1].

### How Stanford Built Silicon Valley
Know the ground you're standing on, because Stanford didn't just end up near Silicon Valley — it more or less created it[cite: 1]. The prime mover was Frederick Terman, the engineering dean and later provost often called the father of Silicon Valley, whose radical idea in the 1930s and '40s was to push his students to start companies near Stanford rather than decamp to the East Coast[cite: 1]. His most famous protégés, Bill Hewlett and Dave Packard, did exactly that: Hewlett-Packard was formalized on January 1, 1939, out of a tiny Palo Alto garage now plaqued as the “Birthplace of Silicon Valley.”[cite: 1] In 1951 Terman went further and created the Stanford Industrial Park (today the Stanford Research Park) — the world's first university research park — leasing Stanford land to firms like Varian and HP and pulling the whole industry to the university's doorstep[cite: 1]. The other genetic strand arrived in 1956, when transistor co-inventor William Shockley set up shop in Mountain View; a year later eight of his best people (the “traitorous eight,” among them Gordon Moore and Robert Noyce) walked out to found Fairchild Semiconductor, and Moore and Noyce later left Fairchild to start Intel[cite: 1]. The silicon those companies etched is why the journalist Don Hoefler popularized the name “Silicon Valley” in print in 1971[cite: 1].

### Sand Hill Road
The venture-capital half of the story is a two-minute drive from the GSB[cite: 1]. Sand Hill Road, running right along the edge of campus, became the most famous concentration of venture capital on earth after Kleiner Perkins opened the first VC office there in 1972 and Sequoia Capital set up nearby the same year[cite: 1]. The pleasing detail: the money and the silicon are the same bloodline — Eugene Kleiner was one of Fairchild's traitorous eight, Tom Perkins came out of HP, and Sequoia's Don Valentine came from Fairchild[cite: 1]. The chip pioneers became the financiers[cite: 1]. The address still carries the weight — Sand Hill has traded places with Manhattan for the most expensive office rents in the country — and the firms behind Amazon, Google, and countless others sit within biking distance of your dorm[cite: 1]. You are at the literal center of this ecosystem; act accordingly[cite: 1].

### Born at Stanford
A partial, humbling list of what has come out of this place[cite: 1]:
- **From the GSB specifically:** Nike (Phil Knight, MBA '62, whose original idea traces to a paper in a GSB small-business class), DoorDash (Tony Xu and Evan Moore, MBA '13, straight out of the Startup Garage course), Charles Schwab (MBA '61), Trulia, StubHub, and Kiva[cite: 1].
- **From the wider university:** Hewlett-Packard, Google (Larry Page and Sergey Brin, CS PhD students), Yahoo, Cisco (built by two Stanford staffers, one of whom ran computing for the GSB), Sun Microsystems (whose name stands for *Stanford University Network*), Netflix (Reed Hastings, CS master's), Instagram, Snapchat, LinkedIn, Robinhood, Nvidia, VMware, and PayPal[cite: 1].

The point isn't to intimidate you — it's that the distance between “student here” and “founder of that” has been crossed many times, from your exact seat[cite: 1].

### 1. Packing and Shipping

#### What to Actually Bring
Pack what is expensive, hard to replace, or needed the day you arrive — plus the personal stuff (photos, small decorations) that makes a dorm room feel like yours[cite: 1]. Nearly everything else can be bought within a 15-minute drive or delivered in two days[cite: 1].

- **Clothes:** The GSB dress code is very relaxed — t-shirts and athleisure are the norm[cite: 1]. Bring formal outfits for interviews and formal GSB events[cite: 1]. The unofficial wardrobe calendar: Halloween costumes in fall; something sparkly for Vegas FOAM in winter; formalwear (long gowns, suits) for the Winter Formal in winter and GSB Prom in spring; and national dress or costumes for the many themed and costume parties in between[cite: 1].
- **A Warm Layer:** Despite the sunny brochures, Palo Alto gets cold from November to February, especially at night[cite: 1]. Pack sweaters and a real coat[cite: 1].
- **Medication and Prescriptions:** Bring a refill of any prescriptions so there's no pharmacy scramble on arrival, and a paper copy of your eyeglass prescription — eye exams in the US are expensive[cite: 1].
- **Sports Gear:** Tahoe is three hours away, Santa Cruz has surf, and the redwoods have world-class trails[cite: 1]. If you own gear you love, bring it — campus sports infrastructure is extensive[cite: 1].
- **Electronics (International Students):** Bring at least one US-mains adapter for day one; consider replacing cheap electronics in the US rather than hauling a bag of adapters[cite: 1].

#### What Your Room Already Has
GSB Residences rooms come with more than you might expect[cite: 1]. Shared kitchens include a two-burner stove, oven, refrigerator/freezer, microwave, garbage disposal, kitchen table and chairs, and a compost bin on request; studios come furnished with the basics[cite: 1]. A room-by-room packing list — split into “provided by GSB Residences,” “ship to school,” and “buy at school” — is in the Resources folder (`GSB Residences Packing List.xlsx`)[cite: 1]. Check what your specific housing includes before buying anything large, and remember on-campus rooms are small — pack accordingly[cite: 1].

#### Shipping and Pre-Arrival Deliveries
- GSB Residences accept packages a few days before move-in (and the front desks process deliveries year-round once you're in); other residences vary — confirm with your front desk before shipping[cite: 1]. Official move-in info lives on MyGSB[cite: 1].
- Linens and towels are not provided for day one[cite: 1]. Order online for delivery to your dorm, or pack one set[cite: 1]. Many students pre-order basics from IKEA for delivery — but at term start IKEA delivery slows down and dorm staples sell out, so order early[cite: 1].
- International shipping via UPS/FedEx/DHL is expensive; check your home-country airlines' cargo divisions (e.g., Lufthansa Cargo, Delta Cargo), or ask an MBA2 or California-based classmate to accept boxes[cite: 1].
- Forgot something? It's fine[cite: 1]. A Stanford email historically gets a free six-month Amazon Prime student trial (terms change — check current), and Chapter 9 has the complete move-in shopping map[cite: 1].
- Shipping a car across the country comes up at both ends of the two years — getting here, and the post-graduation scramble back east[cite: 1]. Classmates have used and recommended **Montway** (praised on price and speed) and **McCollister’s**; get a couple of quotes, since these are brokered and prices swing with the season and the route[cite: 1].

### 2. Getting to Campus

Most people fly into **SFO** (closer, more international routes) or **SJC** (often calmer and closer to the South Bay)[cite: 1]. Your options from the airport, in rough order of popularity:

1. **Uber/Lyft:** SFO to campus runs roughly $40–$100 depending on surge, about 30 minutes without traffic and up to an hour in rush hour[cite: 1]. Download both apps before you land; shared-ride tiers save money when they're offered[cite: 1].
2. **Taxi:** Around $100 plus tip from SFO; rideshare is almost always the better deal[cite: 1].
3. **Public Transit (cheapest, slowest, rough with luggage — 1 hour+):** From SFO, take BART to Millbrae and switch to a southbound Caltrain to Palo Alto station; from SJC, take VTA to Santa Clara station and a northbound Caltrain to Palo Alto[cite: 1]. From the Palo Alto station, the free Marguerite shuttle, a rideshare, or a ~10-minute cab gets you to campus[cite: 1].

- **The Marguerite** is Stanford's free public bus — anyone can hop on, no ID needed[cite: 1]. It connects campus with the Palo Alto Caltrain station and runs several routes you'll use all year (some are handy for groceries)[cite: 1].
- **Caltrain** itself is free for Stanford students through the **GoPass** — one of the best-kept transit secrets on campus[cite: 1].
- If you arrive before your housing contract starts, Airbnb and Stanford's visitor-lodging list cover the gap[cite: 1].
- **Driving to SFO instead?** Long-term parking sits just past the airport with the AirTrain running from the garage to the terminals; spots can be reserved in advance, though walk-ups usually work[cite: 1].

> **Pro Tip:** Arrive early if you possibly can[cite: 1]. Week Zero is a wall of admin, IT setup, and social events; you do not want to be assembling furniture during it[cite: 1]. Aim to have your apartment functional before Welcome Weekend[cite: 1]. If you'll have real furniture to assemble or a genuine move, just outsource it — a TaskRabbit for assembly or a two-person moving crew for a few hundred dollars is well worth skipping the Week-Zero scramble[cite: 1].

#### Pre-MBA Trips (for the Admitted-Student Reader)
- The big three are **Yacht Week**, **Colombia**, and **pre-MBA Tahoe**[cite: 1]. One classmate enjoyed doing all three: it's pretty special to travel with a hundred-plus of your future classmates, and you're simply never going to get that in the real world[cite: 1]. If your summer allows the time, enjoy it before school starts[cite: 1].
- Some **Global Study Trips** also run as pre-MBA options[cite: 1]. They get good reviews for exactly the opposite reason as the big trips: ~30 people instead of several hundred, which is a very good size for finding your first friend group[cite: 1].
- They’re complements, not substitutes: the Yacht Week boats build depth, and so do the Tahoe houses — small, automatic social circles formed around the group you travel with[cite: 1]. Colombia is unstructured breadth: there’s no easy way to make a three-hundred-person group smaller, so you simply meet a lot of people at a rapid rate — and even that makes it easier to say hello again when you cross paths on campus a few weeks later[cite: 1]. One alum’s verdict: the yacht-week core group “anchored me on the Colombia trip as I branched out.”[cite: 1]
- **Logistics learned the hard way:**
  - On **Yacht Week**, get air conditioning and a host on your boat — without a host you will eat terribly all week — and push for all-GSB boats: people who sailed GSB-only boats stayed friends with their boatmates, while mixed boats meant half the crew vanished to HBS afterward[cite: 1]. Yacht Week is where many people find their starting GSB friend group, and it pays off all fall quarter[cite: 1].
  - For **Colombia**, price the operator's add-ons against booking them yourself on Airbnb Experiences or TripAdvisor — the operator's real value is transport and keeping everyone physically together[cite: 1].
  - **Pre-MBA Tahoe** is, in my own opinion, the most skippable of the three, and not because it’s bad: Tahoe is a few hours away and you will go all year (Chapter 25), while Yacht Week and Colombia happen exactly once[cite: 1]. The counterpoint is scale — some classes turn it into a three-hundred-person weekend, which is its own kind of special[cite: 1]. If you go, do the three-to-four-day version rather than the two-day one — it’s three hours each way on a good day and five in traffic, and two days isn’t worth the windshield time[cite: 1].
  - **Small detail:** Bring a Stanford flag — ideally more than one[cite: 1]. Every group photo from Yacht Week and Colombia ends up wanting one[cite: 1].

#### Colombia, Tactically
- The trip runs through an outside operator (VAOVA, in recent years)[cite: 1]. The honest class consensus: you’re paying a premium, and it’s still worth going — what you’re buying is transport, the big parties, and several hundred classmates moving as one group[cite: 1]. Price everything else yourself[cite: 1].
- **Book the parties; skip the packaged dinners.** The parties are the reason to pay[cite: 1]. Dinners you can book yourself at the same restaurants for roughly half the price, in classmates’ experience — and if you want company, post the restaurant in the class chat and people will simply show up[cite: 1].
- **Worth the upgrade:** Your own bedroom[cite: 1]. People run on wildly different schedules — the 4 a.m. crowd and the 8 a.m.-excursion crowd do not mix well in one room — and because it’s Colombia, a single room costs surprisingly little extra[cite: 1]. Skip the premium-hotel upgrade in Cartagena (you can walk over and hang out at that hotel with your friends anyway) and spend the same money on having your own bedroom instead[cite: 1].
- Most day activities are cheaper booked yourself on Airbnb Experiences or TripAdvisor; the catch is rallying people, which is a hassle[cite: 1]. Be honest about the 8 a.m. options, too — plenty of people end up selling their morning slots[cite: 1].
- **Flights:** Book the Avianca legs the minute they’re released — they can disappear within about fifteen minutes[cite: 1]. Affiliate your United status for better boarding and points on the intermediate legs[cite: 1]. Checking in early for a premium seat runs about $18, and first class on the short connecting flights is often only $10–15 more, which is worth it[cite: 1].
- The airport buses leave three to three-and-a-half hours before flights, out of an abundance of caution[cite: 1]. Plenty of people take a cheap Uber an hour later and sleep in instead — but carry that risk knowingly: if you miss the flight, the operator is not going to rebook you[cite: 1].
- **Health:** Drink bottled water everywhere, and in Cartagena use it to brush your teeth as well[cite: 1]. The move on arriving in each city: walk to the nearest grocery or convenience store and buy two of the big jugs for your room, and grab some fruit while you’re there[cite: 1]. Your body will thank you by day four[cite: 1].
- **Packing & Extras:** Bogotá, Medellín, and Cartagena are three different climates, so packing cubes earn their keep[cite: 1]. Bring a fan (Cartagena is hot and humid, and the airport has been known to have no air conditioning), shorts for the white party, a flower lei for the flower party, and cough drops[cite: 1].
- **Conserve your voice:** This is the sleeper tip[cite: 1]. Don’t spend every night shouting over the music — talk to the people next to you instead[cite: 1]. If you aren’t conscious about it, your voice is gone by the second or third city and you’ll croak through the rest of the trip[cite: 1].
- **Two things people rave about:** **Tejo in Medellín** — Colombia’s national game, gunpowder targets and all, which a local guide will set up and teach you (bring cash, around 50,000 COP) — and the **Comuna 13 walking tour**, which is worth doing with a good guide[cite: 1].
- Some classes have run an unofficial **“shadow trek”**: booking the same flights, hotels, and cities themselves and travelling alongside the group[cite: 1]. It’s cheaper, and the trade-off is real — you can’t sign up for the optional activities or the operator’s big parties, so you’re on your own to find people for those[cite: 1].
- On the last day, tip the staff and hand over your leftover pesos before heading to the airport, and thank the local guides and drivers[cite: 1].
- **Operator-Grade Specifics (2025 trip):** For Tejo in Medellín, the outfit at `instagram.com/tejo_in_medellin` runs a casual teaching session — ask for Chris “El Gringo,” an expat who plays competitive Tejo; bring 50,000 COP in cash and get yourself to the courts in El Poblado[cite: 1]. In Cartagena, the grocery store beside the Hotel Estelar covers bottled water and every other essential[cite: 1].

### 3. Housing

#### The Landscape
GSB students live all over: **Schwab Residential Center** and **Jack McDonald Hall (“JMac”)** — together “the GSB Residences” — plus **Escondido Village Graduate Residences (EVGR)** and the mid-rises/low-rises, **Munger**, **Oak Creek** and other subsidized off-campus options, and regular Palo Alto apartments[cite: 1].

- **Didn't get the GSB Residences?** Genuinely fine[cite: 1]. You keep full access to GSB social life and can use the Residences' amenities and study spaces (a quick card-access request opens the buildings — Chapter 9)[cite: 1]. Every amenity in JMac or Schwab is available to residents of both[cite: 1].
- Plenty of students actively prefer EVGR, Munger, or off-campus: more space, fresh air from the GSB bubble, room for a partner or kids (couples housing in the GSB Residences is limited), or lower cost for a slightly longer bike ride[cite: 1]. Munger, EV/EVGR, and Rains are all close to the GSB with strong GSB communities[cite: 1].
- University housing residents get a **Community Associate (CA)** — a returning Stanford student whose job is to help you settle in and build community[cite: 1].

#### Choosing Where to Live, From the Center Outward
Housing runs from the GSB’s own residences outward, and the trade-off is always the same — proximity and serendipity versus space and livability[cite: 1].

- **The GSB Residences (Schwab & Jack McDonald / "JMac"):** Proximity and serendipity: the 1 a.m. common-room conversations are real, every meeting is a two-minute walk, and the peer-mentor culture lives here[cite: 1]. Rooms come better-equipped than you’d guess — a full XL bed with under-bed storage, desk and office chair, lounge chair with ottoman, a TV, a space heater; shared kitchens carry a stove, oven, microwave, fridge, table, and stools[cite: 1]. Costs: you can really only work in your room, temperature complaints happen, and the lottery can screw you[cite: 1]. Every amenity in either building is open to residents of both[cite: 1].
- **Munger:** Trades proximity for livability: a dishwasher (do not underestimate this), bigger rooms, real hosting space, indoor common rooms, closer to the pool and Tresidder, and an MBA2-heavy, cross-school community — though you’ll commute to spontaneous GSB socializing[cite: 1]. Munger and the GSB Residences are the only housing on the entire campus with air conditioning — with the caveat that a Munger quad shares one A/C setting for the whole quad, while GSB Residences rooms control their own[cite: 1].
- **EVGR High-Rises:** The workhorse choice, especially for second years watching budgets: solid big rooms, the EVGR Pub downstairs, and a relaxed feel once you cross the traffic circle — more of the wider grad-student world, a little more sterile[cite: 1].
- **EV Mid-Rises:** The sweet spot for couples without kids: a real step up in space, still close, with a large GSB-couples contingent[cite: 1].
- **Schwab Couples Housing:** The other couples option: unbeatable proximity, but really two Schwab singles joined with one room converted to a living room, at a notably higher price — it is quite expensive[cite: 1].
- **EV Low-Rises:** Built for families with kids and incredible bang for the buck: a little farther out, but a no-brainer with a third or fourth family member coming[cite: 1].
- **Rains:** Older and lower-quality, but its low-rise layout breeds real community and hosts a lot of the university’s grad-student parties[cite: 1].

Housing for your second year is a spring-quarter application (late April/early May) with group mechanics covered in Part III[cite: 1].

#### Pets, Before You Promise the Dog
Stanford student housing is **no-pets, full stop** — and enforcement has teeth: a resident found with an animal is charged **$100 per day** until it's rehomed[cite: 1]. The exceptions are registered service animals and OAE-approved support animals[cite: 1]. If the dog is non-negotiable, R&DE maintains a list of subsidized off-campus apartments that accept pets[cite: 1]. Decide this before you arrive, not after[cite: 1].

#### Neighborhood Profiles (Off-Campus)
*Rent anchors are 2026 one-bedroom averages[cite: 1]:*

- **Downtown Palo Alto (University Ave):** Walk-to-everything option — restaurants, bars, social gravity[cite: 1]. Priciest per square foot ($3,100–$4,000)[cite: 1].
- **California Ave / Midtown Palo Alto:** Quieter, Sunday farmers market, Third Thursdays, fully bikeable to campus[cite: 1]. Default for people who want Palo Alto without University Ave's volume[cite: 1].
- **Menlo Park:** One-bedrooms ~$3,600[cite: 1]. Sand Hill VC corridor on one end, sleepy downtown on the other[cite: 1]. Popular with couples and finance-track students[cite: 1].
- **Mountain View:** One-bedrooms ~$3,500–$3,900[cite: 1]. Newer buildings, amenities, parking[cite: 1]. Castro Street is a real downtown; Caltrain makes campus a straight shot[cite: 1].
- **Redwood City:** One-bedrooms ~$3,400–$3,700[cite: 1]. Best value-to-downtown ratio — revived center, Caltrain stop, 15–20 minutes to campus[cite: 1].
- **East Palo Alto:** Lowest rents within biking distance (IKEA side of the freeway)[cite: 1]. Check specific block walkability and safety data[cite: 1].
- **San Francisco:** Some MBA2s move for spring quarter or second year[cite: 1]. Free Caltrain GoPass makes it a ~1-hour commute[cite: 1]. You trade daily campus serendipity for city life[cite: 1].
- **Passdown Houses (Chapter 27):** Named houses passed down between GSB classes change the math entirely, inheriting a social institution[cite: 1].

### 4. Health: Insurance, Vaccinations, and Prep

#### Cardinal Care — The Default
All students are automatically enrolled in Cardinal Care, Stanford's student health plan (administered through Aetna networks)[cite: 1]. It is comprehensive and expensive (~$635/month), and enrollment is a full-year commitment running through August — no early-cancellation refunds, even if a summer employer covers you[cite: 1]. If you want out, apply for a waiver by the posted deadline (mid-September for autumn entrants); no exceptions are granted[cite: 1].

> **CRITICAL RULE:** Cardinal Care (underwritten by Wellfleet) strictly enforces a referral requirement[cite: 1]. As of 2026, non-emergency specialty and off-campus care **must be referred through Vaden first**, or the claim can be denied[cite: 1]. Unless it's an emergency, start at Vaden or its advice line[cite: 1].

#### Waiving and Comparing Plans
A side-by-side comparison built by students (`Health Insurance – Plan Comparison.xlsx` in Resources folder; 2024–25 figures):

| Metric | Cardinal Care | ISO Care Elite | ISO Care | PSI Plans |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Monthly Premium** | ~$635 | ~$228 | ~$128 | ~$235–$343 |
| **Annual Deductible** | $100 | $40 | $500 | $250 |
| **In-Network Co-insurance** | 100% | 90% | 80% | 80% |
| **Out-of-Pocket Max** | $2,000 | $6,350 | $6,350 | $6,350 |
| **Waiver-Eligible?** | N/A (Default) | Yes | No | TBC (Check) |

**Key Takeaways:**
- Check waiver approval for your specific policy with Stanford before opting out[cite: 1]. Stanford historically offered waivers for ISO Elite Care, but NOT ISO Care[cite: 1].
- **International Students:** Waivers require proof of $50,000 medical-evacuation and $25,000 repatriation coverage[cite: 1]. J-visa holders need a deductible of $500 or less[cite: 1].
- **Exclusions:** Several cheap plans explicitly exclude skiing injuries[cite: 1]. GSB students go to Tahoe constantly, and someone gets hurt every year[cite: 1].
- **Dependents:** The Stanford Student Dependent Health Insurance Plan is opt-in and can ONLY be purchased when you first matriculate — your one chance for your entire degree, barring a qualifying life event[cite: 1].

> **European-Passport Holders Note:** Several classmates waived Cardinal Care in favor of **Chapka Student** (French broker underwritten by AXA)[cite: 1]. Recent pricing ran ~€500/year over age 30 with no copay, no deductible, and no cap on hospitalization[cite: 1]. US network partner is UnitedHealthcare (Stanford Health Care is in-network)[cite: 1]. Verify current Stanford waiver eligibility before relying on this[cite: 1].

#### Vaccinations and Records
- Immunization requirements trigger a registration hold if missing[cite: 1]. As of recently, requirements include: 2 MMR doses (after 1st birthday, at least 28 days apart), full Hepatitis B series plus positive antibody titer, and TB screening[cite: 1]. Deadlines are late July for exemptions[cite: 1].
- International students: bring an English translation of immunization records[cite: 1]. TB testing can be booked at Vaden Health Center (866 Campus Drive, 650-498-2336)[cite: 1].
- Vaden travel clinic can provide travel vaccines needed for GSTs and treks[cite: 1].
